## Method Exploration## The Problem (Simple Terms)

**What you're trying to do:** Wrap 12"×12"×2" foam blocks with aluminum foil + faraday fabric so they're smooth and make good electrical contact when blocks touch.

**The wrapping problem:** When you wrap a rectangular block like a present, you get triangle-shaped bunched up material at the corners (like gift wrap ears). These create bumps that prevent blocks from sitting flush together, creating gaps that let EMF leak through.

**The dilemma:**
- Cut the triangles off → Risk gaps in the shielding
- Fold the triangles flat → Creates bumps that prevent blocks from touching properly
- Neither option gives you smooth, consistent block dimensions

## Material Clarifications

### What is "Aluminum Window Screen Mesh"?

**You're right to question this - I was wrong about aluminum helping with EMF shielding at high frequencies.**

**Aluminum screening:**
- Fine metal mesh (like bug screen for windows)
- Wire diameter: 0.011" (0.28mm)
- Mesh size: 18×16 holes per inch
- Used for: Bug screens, basic RF shielding

**Problem with aluminum alone:**
- **Good for low frequencies** (60 Hz power, AM radio 1 MHz)
- **Poor for high frequencies** (WiFi 2.4/5 GHz, cell phones)
- **Why:** Skin depth at 2.4 GHz in aluminum = 0.0017mm. Single layer of 0.28mm wire works, BUT mesh holes (1.4mm gaps between wires) act as slot antennas at GHz frequencies
- **Mesh hole size = 1.4mm, WiFi wavelength = 125mm** → hole is 1/89th of wavelength, causes ~20-30 dB leakage

**Verdict:** Aluminum mesh alone won't meet your needs. **You DO need proper faraday fabric** (copper-nickel or silver-coated fabric with no holes/gaps).

**Composition:** Copper-nickel-silver coated nylon fabric
- **No mesh holes** - solid conductive surface
- **Shielding:** 80-100 dB at 1-10 GHz (WiFi, cell, Bluetooth)
- **Multiple layers:** Copper (outer) + nickel (bonding) + silver (conductivity)
- **Why it works:** Continuous conductive surface, not a mesh

**You're correct - stick with TitanRF or equivalent solid faraday fabric, not mesh.**

## CNC vs Router - What's the Difference?

**You asked: "What's CNC vs a router? What's better?"**

**Short answer:** CNC is the control system, router is the cutting tool. "CNC router" = router controlled by computer.

**CNC = Computer Numerical Control**
- The BRAIN (computer controls motors, reads G-code, moves cutting tool)
- Can control ANY tool: router, hot wire, laser, plasma cutter, 3D printer
- "CNC" alone doesn't cut anything - it's just the control system

**Router = Spinning cutting bit**
- The MUSCLE (high-speed motor spins cutting bits 10,000-30,000 RPM)
- Removes material by grinding/cutting (like handheld Dremel but bigger)
- Can be manual (handheld) or CNC-controlled (automated)

**Types compared:**

**CNC Hot Wire Cutter:**
- Hot wire (heated nichrome) melts through foam
- ✅ Fast for foam (2-3 min per block)
- ✅ Clean cuts, no dust
- ❌ ONLY cuts - can't mill pockets or channels
- ❌ Can't cut hard materials (HDPE, aluminum)
- **Cost:** $500-800
- **Best for:** Just cutting foam blocks to size

**CNC Router:**
- Spinning bit grinds through material
- ✅ Cuts foam (5-10 min per block)
- ✅ Mills pockets (magnet holes, backing plate recesses)
- ✅ Cuts HDPE/aluminum (backing plates, fixtures)
- ✅ Routes channels (for wiring)
- ✅ One machine, multiple operations
- ❌ Creates dust (need vacuum)
- ❌ Slower for basic foam cutting
- **Cost:** $1,500-3,000
- **Best for:** Precision work (pockets, recesses, multiple materials)

**Which is better?**
- **$1K budget:** Neither (hand-cut foam)
- **$2K budget:** CNC hot wire ($500) - just need consistent foam blocks
- **$5K budget:** CNC router ($2,200) - want magnet pockets + backing plate recesses

**Verdict:** CNC router is MORE CAPABLE but NOT BETTER for simple foam cutting. Hot wire faster for foam-only work.

## Vacuum Bagging - Does It Actually Work?

### Your Skepticism: "Fabric is square trying to wrap a cube - does vacuum bagging work?"

**You're absolutely right to question this. Let me explain the reality:**

**What vacuum bagging DOESN'T do:**
- ❌ Doesn't magically eliminate corner triangles (physics still applies)
- ❌ Doesn't turn a square fabric into a fitted box sleeve
- ❌ Won't fix a bad wrapping pattern

**What vacuum bagging DOES do:**
- ✅ Compresses air pockets and wrinkles OUT of whatever pattern you've wrapped
- ✅ Presses material uniformly against foam (14.7 PSI = 2,116 lbs force on 12"×12" face)
- ✅ Makes glue bonds perfect (no air bubbles, uniform pressure)
- ✅ Ensures aluminum foil is perfectly flat against foam (no crinkles)

**Critical insight: Vacuum bagging is PART 2 of the process, not the whole solution**

**Correct workflow:**
1. **PART 1: Use correct wrapping pattern** (cross pattern, box sleeve, or paneling)
   - This solves the corner triangle problem
   - This determines where overlaps/seams go
   
2. **PART 2: Vacuum bag the wrapped block** 
   - Presses your wrap pattern perfectly smooth
   - Eliminates wrinkles within each face
   - Ensures aluminum foil bonds flat to foam
   - Makes fabric layer bond flat to aluminum

**Example: Cross pattern + vacuum bagging**
```
Step 1: Cut cross pattern (solves corner triangles)
Step 2: Lay aluminum foil on foam, spray 3M 77
Step 3: Fold cross pattern around foam (creates flat faces with edge-to-edge corners)
Step 4: Place wrapped block in vacuum bag
Step 5: Pull vacuum (all faces press perfectly flat, glue cures under pressure)
Step 6: Release vacuum after 10 min - block is perfectly smooth
```

**What you get:**
- Corner design comes from wrap pattern (cross eliminates triangles)
- Surface smoothness comes from vacuum bagging (eliminates wrinkles)
- **Vacuum bagging makes a good wrap pattern PERFECT, but won't fix a bad pattern**

**Real-world results:**
- Without vacuum bag: ±1mm thickness variation across face (wrinkles, air pockets)
- With vacuum bag: ±0.1mm thickness variation (perfectly uniform)

**Cost-benefit for 60 blocks:**
- Vacuum bagging equipment: $400-600
- Time per block: +15 min (10 min vacuum, 5 min bag/unbag)
- Quality improvement: ±1mm → ±0.1mm surface smoothness
- **Worth it?** YES if selling product, MAYBE for personal MVP

## CROSS PATTERN CORNER PERFECTION GUIDE

### The Corner Problem (Even with Cross Pattern)

**You asked: "How to make corners lay perfectly and touch on all sides?"**

**Issue:** Even cross patterns can gap at corners if cut wrong.

**Root cause:** Where two arms meet (the "L" joint), fabric edges must be EXACTLY perpendicular to fold properly.

### Solution: 45° Miter Joint Corner Notches

**Miter joint = Two pieces meet at angle (like picture frame corners)**

```
WRONG (straight corners):        RIGHT (45° notched corners):
        ┌─────┐                          ┌─────┐
        │  2  │                         ╱│  2  │╲
        │     │                        ╱ │     │ ╲
   ┌────┴─────┴────┐              ┌────  ─────  ────┐
   │ 1  │  C  │ 3  │              │ 1  │  C  │ 3  │
   │    │     │    │              │    │     │    │
   └────┬─────┬────┘              └────  ─────  ────┘
        │  4  │                        ╲ │     │ ╱
        │     │                         ╲│  4  │╱
        └─────┘                          └─────┘

Problem: Bunches at corners          Solution: Arms meet edge-to-edge
```

**Miter joint detail (zoomed in at one corner):**
```
Arm 2 (top) meets Arm 1 (left):

BEFORE FOLD (flat pattern):          AFTER FOLD (corner joint):

  Arm 2 edge                           Arm 2 folded up
      ╲                                    │╲
       ╲ 45° notch                         │ ╲ Perfect
        ╲___________                       │  ╲ edge-to-edge
        │           Arm 1                  │   │
        │           edge                   │   │ Arm 1 folded up
        │                                  │   │
       Center                             Foam
       square                             corner

The 45° cut ensures fabric edges          No gap, no overlap,
meet EXACTLY when folded upward           no bunching
```

**Why 45° specifically?**
- Foam block has 90° corners
- Two arms meet at corner = 90° ÷ 2 = 45° each
- Miter joint formula: angle = 90° ÷ number of pieces meeting
- Same concept as cutting baseboards or picture frames

**Cut pattern with measurements (12"×12"×2" block):**
```
         2.5" arm length
              ↓
        ┌─────────┐
        │         │
     ╱──┘    2    └──╲   ← 45° notch, 2.5" deep
    ╱                 ╲
   ┌───────────────────┐
   │                   │
   │    1    C    3    │  ← Center 12.25" × 12.25"
   │                   │  ← Arms 12.25" wide
   └───────────────────┘
    ╲                 ╱
     ╲──┐    4    ┌──╱   ← 45° notch cuts
        │         │
        └─────────┘

Total pattern: 17.25" × 17.25"
8 notches total (2 per arm)
Each notch removes 2.5" × 2.5" triangle
```

**Template dimensions for 12"×12"×2" block:**

**Center square (C):** 12.25" × 12.25" (slightly oversized for wrap)

**Arms (1, 2, 3, 4):**
- Width: 12.25" (matches center)
- Length: 2.5" (2" height + 0.5" overlap)

**Corner notches (THE CRITICAL PART):**
- **45° angle cut** at each junction where arm meets center
- Notch size: Cut from corner point 2.5" inward along BOTH edges
- Result: 8 total notches (2 per arm)

**Why 45°?** When you fold arm upward, the notch creates a perfect miter joint (like picture frame corners).

### Perfect Folding Sequence

**Even perfect cuts fail if you fold wrong. Here's the order:**

```
Step 1: Spray 3M 77 on foam        Step 2: Center foam on aluminum
        
        [FOAM BLOCK]               Aluminum foil cross beneath foam
                                   (shiny side UP touches foam)

Step 3: Fold OPPOSITE arms first   Step 4: Fold remaining arms
        
        Fold LEFT (arm 1)          Fold TOP (arm 2)
        then RIGHT (arm 3)         then BOTTOM (arm 4)
        
        This keeps symmetry        All corners now meet perfectly

Step 5: BackBlockFace overlap      Step 6: Aluminum tape sealing
        
        4 arm tips overlap         Apply 2" aluminum tape
        at center (2"×2" area)     over all 4 arm seams
```

**Corner technique (the detail that matters):**

1. As you fold each arm UP, **press the notched corner flat immediately**
2. Use squeegee, credit card edge, or gloved hand
3. Adjacent arm's notch should meet THIS arm's notch edge-to-edge
4. If gap >0.5mm, your notch angle is wrong (check template)

**Pro tip:** Fold aluminum foil layer FIRST (practice run), THEN repeat with fabric layer on top. This way you see any corner gaps before committing fabric.
