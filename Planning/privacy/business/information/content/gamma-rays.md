Short answer
No. Ambient gamma rays from Earth or a nuclear plant cannot be used to “observe” or track people. Flux is too low, signals are diffuse, and nuclear plants are heavily shielded with off‑site emissions far below background.

Why it can’t track people
Imaging needs a source + detector geometry and significant photon counts; background gamma is weak and unstructured.
Nuclear plants: Primary gamma is contained; regulatory limits make off‑site fields tiny relative to natural background—no usable contrast for imaging humans.
Tracking works only if the person/source is radioactive (e.g., carrying a gamma emitter). Then detectors can localize at short distances, but this is detecting the source itself, not imaging a person.

How fast do they travel?
Gamma rays travel at the speed of light.

Gamma rays are highly penetrating. They can easily pass through materials that would stop other radiation, such as paper, wood, and skin.


To stop or attenuate (reduce) them, you need very dense materials. The most common shielding materials are:

Thick lead

Thick concrete

🌍 Can they be produced on Earth?
Yes, they are produced on Earth both naturally and artificially.

Nuclear Reactions: They are a primary product of nuclear fission (in nuclear reactors) and nuclear fusion (in weapons).

Radioactive Decay: Many radioactive isotopes, such as cobalt-60 used in medical radiation therapy, release gamma rays as they decay.

Lightning: A rare, high-altitude phenomenon called a "terrestrial gamma-ray flash" (TGF) can be created by lightning.
